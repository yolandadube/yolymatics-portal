DC & AC

Direct Current  ·  Alternating Current  ·  Generation

✦ Prepared by   Yolanda Dube
Concept 01

DIRECT
CURRENT (DC)

Direct Current (DC) is an electric current that flows in one direction only — always from the negative terminal to the positive terminal of a source.

The flow of electrons is steady and constant — it does not change direction and does not reverse.

DC is produced by sources such as batteries, solar cells, and fuel cells. It was championed by Thomas Edison in the 1880s during the famous "War of Currents."

Current flows in one fixed direction
Electrons flow from negative (−) to positive (+) terminal
Voltage remains constant over time
Produced by: batteries, solar panels, rectifiers
Used in: electronics, phones, cars, computers
DC CIRCUIT — ONE-WAY FLOW
🔋
BATTERY — DC SOURCE
A battery converts chemical energy into electrical energy. It always pushes current in the same direction — from its negative terminal around the circuit to the positive.
AC CIRCUIT — REVERSING FLOW
⚙️
GENERATOR — AC SOURCE
A rotating generator (alternator) produces AC by spinning a coil in a magnetic field. As the coil rotates, current flows first one way, then reverses — producing the characteristic sine wave.
Concept 02

ALTERNATING
CURRENT (AC)

Alternating Current (AC) is an electric current that periodically reverses direction — electrons flow forward, stop, then flow backward in a repeating cycle.

The number of complete cycles per second is the frequency, measured in Hertz (Hz). Most countries use 50 Hz or 60 Hz. South Africa uses 50 Hz.

AC is produced by electrical generators at power stations and is the type of electricity supplied to homes and businesses through the power grid.

Current reverses direction periodically
Forms a sine wave pattern over time
Frequency: 50 Hz (SA) or 60 Hz (USA)
Produced by: alternators / generators
Used in: home power supply, industry, motors

Concept 03

ADVANTAGES
OF AC OVER DC

Nikola Tesla championed AC in the 1880s, ultimately winning the "War of Currents" against Edison's DC. Here is why AC became the global standard for power transmission and distribution:

The single greatest advantage is the ability to use transformers — devices that work only with AC and allow voltage to be stepped up or down easily. This makes long-distance power transmission far more efficient.

⚡ Historical note: Nikola Tesla (AC) vs Thomas Edison (DC) — the "War of Currents" (1880s–1890s). Tesla and Westinghouse's AC system won because of its ability to transmit power over long distances with low losses.
01
TRANSFORMER COMPATIBLE
AC voltage can be stepped up or down using transformers. DC cannot use transformers. This is AC's single biggest advantage.
02
EFFICIENT LONG-DISTANCE TRANSMISSION
High-voltage AC transmission (e.g. 400 kV) means very low current and minimal power loss (P_loss = I²R) over hundreds of kilometres.
03
CHEAPER TO GENERATE
AC generators (alternators) are simpler and less expensive to build and maintain than DC generators. No need for commutators.
04
POWERS AC MOTORS DIRECTLY
Most industrial and household motors run directly on AC. No conversion is needed — fans, pumps, washing machines all use AC motors.
05
EASY VOLTAGE REGULATION
Voltage can be regulated easily at multiple stages — from the power station to your home socket — with transformer substations along the route.
Applications 01

WHERE IS
DC USED?

DC is essential wherever a steady, reliable voltage is needed — especially in electronic devices and portable equipment.

Most devices that run on AC power actually convert it to DC internally using a rectifier circuit — this is what your phone charger does.

Modern developments like HVDC (High Voltage DC) transmission are now challenging AC over very long distances, and electric vehicles have made DC more prominent than ever.

🔋 Key fact: The USB standard delivers DC at 5V (or up to 20V for fast charging). Your laptop, phone, tablet, and earbuds all run on DC internally.

Applications 02

WHERE IS
AC USED?

AC is the standard for power distribution worldwide — from the power station right to the socket in your home. Everything plugged into a wall socket runs on AC.

Electric motors that power fans, air conditioners, washing machines, and industrial equipment are designed specifically for AC because AC naturally creates a rotating magnetic field.

South Africa's national grid (Eskom) generates and distributes 230V / 50 Hz AC to all homes and businesses across the country.

🌍 Global standard: Virtually every country in the world uses AC for its national electricity grid. Only the voltage and frequency differ (50 Hz vs 60 Hz, 110V vs 230V).

INSIDE THE GENERATOR
Rotating coil → AC sine wave output
FARADAY'S LAW
A changing magnetic flux through a coil induces an EMF (voltage). As the coil rotates in the magnetic field, the flux changes sinusoidally — producing a sine-wave AC output.
Concept 04

ELECTROMAGNETIC
INDUCTION

Electricity is generated using Faraday's Law of Electromagnetic Induction — discovered by Michael Faraday in 1831.

When a coil of wire rotates inside a magnetic field, the magnetic flux through the coil continuously changes. This changing flux induces a voltage (EMF) in the coil.

As the coil completes each rotation, the induced voltage alternates between positive and negative — producing the characteristic AC sine wave.

1
Turbine spins the shaft of the generator
2
Shaft rotates a coil inside a strong magnetic field
3
Changing magnetic flux induces EMF in the coil (Faraday's Law)
4
As the coil rotates 360°, the EMF traces one complete sine wave cycle
5
AC electricity exits via slip rings and enters the power grid
Concept 05

TRANSFORMERS

A transformer is a device that changes the voltage of AC electricity using electromagnetic induction.

It works ONLY with AC — DC cannot be transformed because DC produces no changing magnetic flux.

Two coils (primary and secondary) are wound around an iron core. AC in the primary creates a changing magnetic field, which induces a voltage in the secondary.

The voltage ratio equals the turns ratio:

Vp / Vs = Np / Ns
Vp = primary voltage  ·  Vs = secondary voltage
Np = primary turns  ·  Ns = secondary turns
Step-up transformer: Ns > Np → voltage increases (used at power stations)
Step-down transformer: Ns < Np → voltage decreases (used at substations)
Power is conserved: Vp × Ip = Vs × Is (ideal transformer)
STEP-UP TRANSFORMER

CORE PRIMARY 3 turns SECONDARY 5 turns Vp AC IN Vs AC OUT
V_out / V_in = N_s / N_p
Example: If N_p = 100 turns, N_s = 1000 turns → voltage increases 10×  (Step-Up)
If N_s = 10 turns → voltage decreases 10×  (Step-Down)
⬆ STEP-UP
N_s > N_p → Voltage rises. Used at power stations to boost voltage for transmission. Current drops.
⬇ STEP-DOWN
N_s < N_p → Voltage falls. Used at substations to reduce to safe household levels. Current rises.
