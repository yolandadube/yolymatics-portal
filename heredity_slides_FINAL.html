02 / 14 It's All in the Cells
Foundation

Living Things &
Their Cells

All living things — bacteria, plants, animals — share one fundamental thing: they are all made of cells. Cells are life's smallest building blocks.

Inside each cell are organelles — specialised structures that each do a specific job. The most important for heredity is the nucleus: the cell's control centre, where genetic information is stored.

Key organelles: Nucleus (genetic control), Mitochondria (energy), Ribosomes (protein building), Cell membrane (gatekeeper), Endoplasmic reticulum (transport).

03 / 14 DNA — The Blueprint of Life
Structure & Function

DNA: Life's
Master Code

Structure: In the nucleus, chromosomes are made of DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) — a long double-helix molecule tightly coiled around proteins called histones. Think of thread wound around tiny spools.

Function: Each strand of DNA encodes thousands of proteins. The unique arrangement of proteins creates the blueprint for life — determining traits, development, and behaviour.

🧠 If you stretched out all the DNA in one human cell, it would be 2 metres long — yet it fits inside a nucleus just 6 micrometres wide!

06 / 14 Dominant & Recessive Traits
Genetics

Dominant, Recessive, Genotype & Phenotype

Heredity works like rock, paper, scissors — some traits dominate over others.

💪

Dominant Allele

Written as a capital letter (e.g. B). Expressed if the organism has at least one dominant allele. It "wins" over recessive.

🤫

Recessive Allele

Written as a lowercase letter (e.g. b). Only expressed when the organism has two recessive alleles. It "hides" behind dominant.

🔀

Alleles

Different versions of the same gene. Each parent passes one allele per gene to their offspring. Alleles are what create variation between individuals.

🧬 Genotype

The actual genetic code an organism carries — the specific allele combination. Cannot be seen just by looking.

Examples: BB · Bb · bb

👁️ Phenotype

The observable physical appearance that results from the genotype. What you actually see.

Examples: Brown fur · Blue eyes · Curly hair · Tall plant

07 / 14 Punnett Squares
Guinea Pig Example

Predicting Offspring Traits

When two Bb guinea pig parents (black fur, carrying one recessive allele each) have offspring, the Punnett square predicts the outcome.

Reading the results:

• BB (1 offspring) — Black fur, cannot pass white allele

• Bb (2 offspring) — Black fur, carries white allele

• bb (1 offspring) — White fur (recessive shown!)

🐹

Phenotype ratio: 3 Black : 1 White — On average, 75% will be black, 25% white. This is a probability, not a guarantee!

09 / 14 Sex Chromosomes
Pair 23

XX vs XY — Determining Biological Sex

Humans have 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs. The 23rd pair is the sex chromosome pair, and it determines biological sex.

Key fact:

Biological sex is determined by the father's sperm — whether it carries an X or Y chromosome.

All eggs:

Every egg cell from the mother contains only one X chromosome.

Probability:

Each pregnancy has a roughly 50/50 chance of producing a male or female offspring.

10 / 14 Hereditary Diseases
🩸 Hereditary Disease

Sickle Cell Anaemia

A blood disease caused by abnormally shaped red blood cells. Normal cells are round and flexible — sickle cells are long, curved, and rigid.

These crescent-shaped cells get stuck in narrow blood vessels, blocking oxygen flow and causing severe pain attacks called sickle cell crises.

Inheritance Pattern: Follows a recessive pattern. A child must inherit two recessive alleles (one from each parent) to develop the disease. Carrying one allele makes you a carrier — no disease, but you can pass it on.
Symptoms

Pain episodes, anaemia, organ damage, fatigue, increased infection risk

Treatment

Not curable, but managed with medication, hydration, and bone marrow transplants

11 / 14 Hereditary Diseases
Other Hereditary Conditions

Diabetes & Acne

Not all hereditary conditions are simple dominant/recessive patterns — many involve a complex interplay of genes and environment.

💉

Diabetes

Type 1: Strongly genetic — an autoimmune disease where the body attacks its own insulin-producing cells. Having an affected parent increases a child's risk.

Type 2: Influenced by both genes and lifestyle. Genetic predisposition increases risk, but diet and exercise are powerful modifiers.

Key insight: You may inherit a risk, not the disease itself. Healthy choices can prevent Type 2 diabetes even with genetic risk.
🧴

Acne

Research shows acne has a hereditary component. If both parents had severe acne, children are significantly more likely to develop it too. Genes influence:

  • Skin oil (sebum) production levels
  • Pore size and skin cell turnover
  • Inflammatory immune response
Environmental triggers: stress, diet, hormonal changes, and hygiene all influence severity — even with genetic predisposition.
💡

Big Picture: For most complex diseases, heredity sets the stage, but lifestyle choices determine how the story plays out.

12 / 14 Nature vs Nurture
Personal Health

It's Not All in the Genes! 🌿

While you cannot change your DNA, your daily choices and environment powerfully shape your health. Here's what you CAN control:

🧼

Personal Hygiene

Washing hands and good hygiene habits reduce risk of illness, infection, and disease transmission significantly.

🥦

Diet & Exercise

Eating a balanced diet and exercising regularly lowers obesity risk and reduces chance of Type 2 diabetes and heart disease.

☀️

Sun Protection

Limiting UV exposure, especially 10 am–3 pm, significantly reduces risk of skin cancer.

🌿

Clean Environment

Avoiding smoke, radiation, and pollution decreases risk of developing cancers and respiratory diseases.

Your Health = Nature + Nurture

13 / 14 Key Terms
Vocabulary Review

Key Terms to Know

Heredity

The passing of traits from parents to offspring through genes.

DNA

Deoxyribonucleic acid — the molecule that carries all genetic instructions.

Chromosome

Tightly coiled DNA + protein structures in the nucleus. Humans have 46.

Gene

A specific segment of DNA that codes for a particular protein/trait.

Allele

Different versions of the same gene (e.g. B for black fur, b for white).

Dominant

An allele that is expressed when at least one copy is present (capital letter).

Recessive

An allele only expressed when two copies are present (lowercase letter).

Genotype

The actual genetic code an organism carries. Cannot be directly observed.

Phenotype

The observable physical appearance resulting from an organism's genotype.
