Focus Question

ENERGY,
MOTION &
TEMPERATURE

What is the interaction between kinetic energy, motion, and the temperature of particles?

Moving from a warm climate to a cold one changes the pressure inside your car tyres — even though the amount of gas stays the same. The molecules inside simply changed how they move and interact.

To understand how particles behave under different conditions, we use the Kinetic Theory of Matter.

🔑 Key idea: Temperature is a measure of how fast particles are moving. Faster particles = higher temperature = more kinetic energy.
THE TYRE PRESSURE ANALOGY
🌞 WARM CLIMATE
Gas molecules inside the tyre have high kinetic energy — they move fast and collide with the tyre walls frequently and forcefully → higher pressure.
↓
❄ COLD CLIMATE
Same gas, but molecules now have less kinetic energy — they move more slowly and collide less forcefully with the walls → lower pressure. Warning light!
💡 KEY INSIGHT
The amount of gas didn't change — but the motion of molecules (kinetic energy) changed with temperature. This is exactly what kinetic theory explains.
Concept 01

KINETIC
THEORY
FOUNDATIONS

The kinetic theory of matter is built on a set of foundational assumptions — ideas we accept as true — that help explain how all matter behaves at the particle level.

These assumptions connect the microscopic world (individual particles) to macroscopic observations (temperature, pressure, volume) that we can measure.

🔍 Remember: "Kinetic" comes from the Greek word kinesis meaning motion. Kinetic energy is the energy of movement.
THE THREE CORE ASSUMPTIONS
1
MATTER IS MADE OF TINY PARTICLES
All matter is composed of very small particles such as ions, atoms, and molecules — too small to see with the naked eye, but responsible for all the properties we observe.
2
PARTICLES ARE ARRANGED BY STATE
The particles in matter are organised in different arrangements depending on the state of matter: solid, liquid, gas, or plasma. Arrangement determines the material's properties.
3
PARTICLES ARE IN CONSTANT MOTION
Particles are always moving. They have different amounts of kinetic energy and move in various ways — vibrating, rotating, or translating — depending on the state of matter.

TEMPERATURE = AVERAGE KE
At any moment, individual particles have different amounts of kinetic energy. Temperature is always the average kinetic energy of all particles in the system.
Concept 02

KE, MOTION
& TEMPERATURE

The temperature of any substance is determined by the average kinetic energy of its particles.

Hotter objects have faster-moving particles — more KE.
Cooler objects have slower-moving particles — less KE.

Individual particles within the same substance can have more or less energy than their neighbours — temperature is always the average of all.

Temperature measures average KE — not total, not individual
A thermometer works by absorbing heat energy from surroundings — warm molecules transfer KE to the fluid, which expands and rises in the tube
Heat flows from hot (high KE) to cold (low KE) until equilibrium
At absolute zero (0 K / −273°C) particle motion stops completely — minimum possible KE
THE FOUR STATES OF MATTER
Particle arrangement and motion change with kinetic energy
SOLID
LOW KE
Tightly packed in fixed positions. Particles only vibrate in place. Definite shape & volume. Strong intermolecular forces.
LIQUID
MEDIUM KE
Close but mobile — particles flow past each other. Definite volume, no fixed shape. Weaker forces than solids.
GAS
HIGH KE
Far apart, fast moving — particles fly freely in all directions. No fixed shape or volume. Very weak forces.
PLASMA
VERY HIGH KE
Ionised gas — atoms stripped of electrons. Found in stars, lightning, and neon signs. Most common state in the universe.
⬆️ Moving from solid → liquid → gas → plasma: kinetic energy increases, particle spacing increases, intermolecular forces weaken.

PROPERTIES OF GASES
Basic characteristics under normal conditions
🫧
LOW DENSITY
Gas particles are far apart from one another, so the density of a gas is very low compared to the same substance as a liquid or solid. The same mass occupies far more volume in the gas phase.
🗜️
COMPRESSIBILITY
Gases can be compressed into a smaller volume because there is so much empty space between the particles. Liquids and solids resist compression because particles are already close together.
↔️
EXPANSION
Gases expand to fill their container completely — they have no fixed shape or volume. This is because gas particles are in constant rapid random motion with little attraction to each other.
🌫️
DIFFUSION
Gases spread out and mix spontaneously from high to low concentration — this is why you can smell food cooking from across the room. Faster (hotter) molecules diffuse more quickly.
🌊
FLUIDITY
Like liquids, gases can flow freely — they are fluids. Gas particles can move past each other easily because intermolecular forces are negligible. This is why wind blows and fans work.
💡 Under extremely high pressures or very low temperatures, gases may deviate from these normal properties and begin to behave more like liquids — this is where the ideal gas model breaks down.
MEASURING GASES
Four variables needed to fully describe a gas sample
V
VOLUME
Litres (L)
The space the gas occupies. Gas fills its entire container, so V = container volume. Also measured in mL, dm³, or m³.
P
PRESSURE
Atmospheres (atm)
Force per unit area exerted by gas particles colliding with container walls. SI unit is the Pascal (Pa = N/m²).
T
TEMPERATURE
Kelvin (K)
Always use Kelvin in gas calculations! K = °C + 273. At 0 K (absolute zero), all particle motion stops.
n
AMOUNT
Moles (mol)
The number of particles in the gas sample, expressed in moles. 1 mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's number).
⚡ What is gas pressure? When gas particles move randomly and collide with the walls of their container, each collision exerts a tiny force. The sum of billions of these collisions per second creates what we measure as pressure. More particles, higher temperature, or smaller volume = more collisions = higher pressure.

PRESSURE UNITS
Pa = N/m²
The Pascal is the SI unit of pressure. Named after Blaise Pascal.
🔬 Pa (Pascal) — used in physics and SI calculations
🧪 atm (atmosphere) — most common in chemistry, based on Earth's atmospheric pressure at sea level
🌡️ mm Hg (torr) — used in medicine (blood pressure) and meteorology
🌍 kPa (kilopascal) — used in meteorology and engineering
Standard atmospheric pressure (STP) = 1 atm = 760 mm Hg = 101.3 kPa
PRESSURE CONVERSIONS
Using dimensional analysis — always show the conversion factor
Worked Example
785 mm Hg → atm
785 mm Hg × (1 atm / 760 mm Hg)
= 785 ÷ 760 atm
= 1.03 atm ✓
Worked Example
654 torr → atm
654 torr × (1 atm / 760 torr)
= 654 ÷ 760 atm
= 0.861 atm ✓
Worked Example
111 kPa → atm
111 kPa × (1 atm / 101.3 kPa)
= 111 ÷ 101.3 atm
= 1.10 atm ✓
Key Method — Dimensional Analysis
GENERAL RULE
To convert TO atm: divide by the unit factor
To convert FROM atm: multiply by the unit factor

Unit factors:
mm Hg ↔ atm: ÷ 760 or × 760
torr ↔ atm: ÷ 760 or × 760
kPa ↔ atm: ÷ 101.3 or × 101.3
📝 Exam tip: Always write out your conversion factor as a fraction. The unit you're converting FROM must appear in the denominator so it cancels out, leaving only the unit you want.
KINETIC MOLECULAR THEORY OF GASES
5 Postulates for the Ideal Gas Model
1
CONSTANT RANDOM MOTION
The particles of a gas are in constant, rapid, random motion — they move in straight lines until they collide with something. There is no preferred direction.
2
LARGE NUMBERS OF TINY PARTICLES
Gases consist of enormous numbers of extremely tiny particles (atoms or molecules) with negligible volume compared to the overall volume of the gas.
3
ELASTIC COLLISIONS
Collisions between gas particles (and with container walls) are perfectly elastic — total kinetic energy is conserved. No energy is lost to heat during a collision.
4
NO INTERMOLECULAR FORCES
There are no forces of attraction or repulsion between gas particles except during the brief moment of collision. Particles are completely independent of each other.
5
KE IS PROPORTIONAL TO TEMPERATURE
The average kinetic energy of gas particles is directly proportional to their absolute temperature (in Kelvin). Double the temperature → double the average KE. KE_avg ∝ T
🔬 Ideal Gas Model: A gas that follows ALL five postulates exactly. In reality, most real gases deviate slightly (especially at high pressures or low temperatures) — but the ideal model is an excellent starting point for predictions and calculations.
LESSON RECAP
⚛️
Kinetic Theory
Matter = tiny particles in constant motion. Arrangement and motion differ by state. Kinetic energy determines temperature.
🌡️
Temperature & KE
Temperature = average kinetic energy of ALL particles. Hot = fast. Cold = slow. Thermometers measure heat transfer from surroundings.
🧊
States of Matter
Solid (vibrate, fixed) → Liquid (flow, close) → Gas (fast, far apart) → Plasma (ionised). Each state has distinct particle arrangement and KE.
💨
Gas Properties
Low density · Compressible · Expands to fill container · Diffuses · Flows. Described by V (L), P (atm), T (K), n (mol).
📐
Pressure Units
1 atm = 760 mm Hg = 760 torr = 101.3 kPa = 101 325 Pa. Use dimensional analysis to convert. Always show the conversion factor.
🔬
KMT — 5 Postulates
① Random motion ② Tiny particles ③ Elastic collisions ④ No intermolecular forces ⑤ KE ∝ Temperature. Describes the ideal gas.
