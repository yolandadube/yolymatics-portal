Concept 01

WHAT IS
RESISTANCE?

Electrical resistance is the opposition that a material offers to the flow of electric current.

Think of it like friction — just as friction slows down a moving object, resistance slows down the flow of electrons through a conductor.

The more resistance, the harder it is for current to flow — and the more energy is lost as heat.

Symbol: R
Unit: Ohm (Ω) — named after Georg Simon Ohm
1 Ω = 1 volt per ampere (V/A)
Conductors (metals) have low resistance
Insulators (rubber, wood) have very high resistance
🌊
WATER ANALOGY
Water pipes with narrow passages resist flow. Wider pipe → less resistance → more flow. A partially blocked pipe = resistor.
⚡
ELECTRIC CIRCUIT
Electrons collide with atoms as they flow through a wire. More collisions = more resistance. A thin wire resists more than a thick wire.
Ω
THE OHM
Named after Georg Simon Ohm (1789–1854). The unit of resistance. 1 Ω means a voltage of 1V drives a current of 1A through the component.
ρ
RESISTIVITY (rho)
An intrinsic property of a material — it describes how strongly that material opposes current flow, regardless of its size or shape.

Concept 02

RESISTIVITY
ρ (RHO)

Resistivity is a property of the material itself — not the shape or size of the object.

Two wires made of the same material have the same resistivity — even if one is long and thin, and the other is short and fat.

Resistivity depends on temperature — for most metals, resistivity increases with temperature (more atomic vibrations = more collisions).

Good conductors have very low resistivity (10⁻⁸ range)
Insulators have very high resistivity (10¹⁰ to 10¹⁶)
Semiconductors fall in between — their resistivity can be controlled
Resistivity is an intrinsic property — like density or melting point

ρ = RA / L   →  find resistivity
L = RA / ρ   →  find length

💡 Key insight: Longer wire = more resistance  |  Thicker wire = less resistance  |  Different material = different ρ
4 FACTORS AFFECTING RESISTANCE
📏
LENGTH
R ∝ L
Longer wire → more collisions between electrons and atoms → more resistance. Double the length = double the resistance.
⭕
AREA
R ∝ 1/A
Thicker wire → more pathways for electrons → less resistance. Double the area = half the resistance.
🔬
MATERIAL
R ∝ ρ
Different materials have different atomic structures. Some (copper) let electrons flow freely. Others (nichrome) oppose flow much more — higher ρ.
🌡️
TEMPERATURE
R ∝ T (metals)
Higher temperature → atoms vibrate more → more electron collisions → more resistance in most metals. Opposite in semiconductors.
📐 The formula R = ρL/A captures three of these four factors. Temperature effects are accounted for by noting that ρ changes with temperature.
Focus Question — How does voltage, current, and resistance relate?
OHM'S LAW

The voltage (potential difference) across a conductor is directly proportional to the current through it — provided temperature and physical conditions remain constant.
Twice the voltage → twice the current. Simple. Powerful.
👨‍🔬 Georg Simon Ohm (German physicist, 1789–1854) published this law in 1827, originally met with ridicule. He showed that V / I = constant for a given conductor at constant temperature. That constant IS the resistance.
THE OHM'S LAW TRIANGLE
Cover the variable you want to find — the remaining two show the formula!
THREE FORMS OF OHM'S LAW
V = I × R
Find Voltage — when current and resistance are known.
Units: I in Amps × R in Ohms = V in Volts ✓
I = V / R
Find Current — when voltage and resistance are known.
Units: V in Volts ÷ R in Ohms = I in Amps ✓
R = V / I
Find Resistance — when voltage and current are known.
Units: V in Volts ÷ I in Amps = R in Ohms ✓
⚠️ Always check units first — convert kΩ to Ω, mA to A, before substituting into the formula!
Concept 04

THE V–I
GRAPH

For an ohmic conductor — one that obeys Ohm's Law — the graph of voltage (V) against current (I) is a straight line through the origin.

The gradient (slope) of this line equals the resistance R.

A steeper line = higher resistance.
A flatter line = lower resistance.

Gradient = ΔV / ΔI = R
Line through origin → V = 0 when I = 0 ✓
Non-ohmic devices (bulbs, diodes) give curved lines
Ohmic: resistors, metallic conductors at constant temperature
WORKED EXAMPLES — OHM'S LAW
Example 1 — Find Voltage
A resistor has a resistance of 20 Ω. A current of 3 A flows through it. Calculate the voltage across it.
Given: R = 20 Ω  ·  I = 3 A  ·  Find: V = ?
▶ Formula: V = I × R
▶ Substitute: V = 3 × 20
V = 60 V ✓
Example 2 — Find Resistance
A voltage of 12 V is applied across a component. A current of 0.5 A flows. Calculate the resistance.
Given: V = 12 V  ·  I = 0.5 A  ·  Find: R = ?
▶ Formula: R = V / I
▶ Substitute: R = 12 / 0.5
R = 24 Ω ✓
Example 3 — Find Current
A lamp has a resistance of 880 Ω and is connected to a 220 V supply. What current flows through it?
Given: R = 880 Ω  ·  V = 220 V  ·  Find: I = ?
▶ Formula: I = V / R
▶ Substitute: I = 220 / 880
I = 0.25 A ✓
Example 4 — Unit Conversion
A 9 V battery powers a circuit with resistance 1.5 kΩ. Find the current in milliamperes.
Given: V = 9 V  ·  R = 1.5 kΩ = 1 500 Ω  ·  Find: I in mA
▶ Convert: 1.5 kΩ = 1 500 Ω
▶ I = V/R = 9 / 1 500 = 0.006 A
▶ Convert: 0.006 A × 1000 = 6 mA
I = 6 mA ✓
WORKED EXAMPLES — RESISTIVITY
Example 5 — Find Resistance
A copper wire is 2 m long with cross-section 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ m². (ρ_copper = 1.72 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m). Find R.
ρ = 1.72 × 10⁻⁸  ·  L = 2 m  ·  A = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ m²
▶ R = ρL / A
▶ R = (1.72 × 10⁻⁸ × 2) / (1.0 × 10⁻⁶)
▶ R = 3.44 × 10⁻⁸ / 1.0 × 10⁻⁶
R = 0.0344 Ω ✓
Example 6 — Find Length
A nichrome wire has R = 10 Ω and A = 5.0 × 10⁻⁷ m². (ρ_nichrome = 1.10 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m). Find L.
R = 10 Ω  ·  A = 5.0 × 10⁻⁷ m²  ·  ρ = 1.10 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m
▶ Rearrange: L = RA / ρ
▶ L = (10 × 5.0 × 10⁻⁷) / (1.10 × 10⁻⁶)
▶ L = 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ / 1.10 × 10⁻⁶
L = 4.55 m ✓
Example 7 — Find Resistivity
A 3 m wire with A = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ m² has R = 0.45 Ω. Calculate the resistivity of the material.
L = 3 m  ·  A = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ m²  ·  R = 0.45 Ω
▶ Rearrange: ρ = RA / L
▶ ρ = (0.45 × 2.0 × 10⁻⁶) / 3
▶ ρ = 9.0 × 10⁻⁷ / 3
ρ = 3.0 × 10⁻⁷ Ω·m ✓
🔑 3-Step Method:  ① Write the formula  →  ② Rearrange for the unknown  →  ③ Substitute values + check units
PRACTICE PROBLEMS — OHM'S LAW
Try these on your own first — then check your answer
P1 — Find Voltage
A resistor of 15 Ω has a current of 4 A flowing through it. Calculate the voltage across the resistor.
Given: R = 15 Ω  ·  I = 4 A
HINT: Use V = I × R
Answer: 60 V
P2 — Find Current
A 230 V mains supply is connected to a heater of resistance 1 150 Ω. Calculate the current through the heater.
Given: V = 230 V  ·  R = 1 150 Ω
HINT: Use I = V / R
Answer: 0.2 A
P3 — Find Resistance
A 6 V battery drives a current of 30 mA (0.030 A) through a resistor. What is the resistance?
Given: V = 6 V  ·  I = 0.030 A
HINT: Use R = V / I
Answer: 200 Ω
P4 — Unit Conversion
A resistor has 15 V across it and draws a current of 50 mA. Find its resistance. Express in kΩ.
Given: V = 15 V  ·  I = 50 mA = 0.050 A
HINT: Find R in Ω, then convert to kΩ (÷ 1000)
Answer: 300 Ω = 0.3 kΩ
★ P5 — CHALLENGE
A circuit has two resistors in series: R₁ = 120 Ω and R₂ = 80 Ω. The supply voltage is 24 V. (a) Find the total resistance. (b) Find the current. (c) Find the voltage across each resistor.
Remember: In series, R_total = R₁ + R₂  ·  same current flows through both
HINT: (a) R_total = 200 Ω  ·  (b) I = 24/200  ·  (c) V₁ = IR₁, V₂ = IR₂
Answers: (a) 200 Ω  (b) 0.12 A  (c) V₁ = 14.4 V, V₂ = 9.6 V
📝 Exam strategy: Always write ① What is given ② The formula ③ Substitution ④ Answer + unit. Never skip steps — marks are awarded for method!
PRACTICE PROBLEMS — RESISTIVITY
R = ρL / A  ·  remember: check all units first!
R1 — Copper Wire
An aluminium wire (ρ = 2.82 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m) is 5 m long with a circular cross-section of diameter 1 mm. Find its resistance.
d = 1 mm = 0.001 m  →  r = 0.0005 m  →  A = πr² = π(0.0005)² = 7.85 × 10⁻⁷ m²
HINT: R = ρL/A = (2.82 × 10⁻⁸ × 5) / (7.85 × 10⁻⁷)
Answer: R ≈ 0.179 Ω
R2 — Find Length
What length of copper wire (ρ = 1.72 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m) with cross-section 4.0 × 10⁻⁷ m² is needed to make a resistance of 0.5 Ω?
R = 0.5 Ω  ·  A = 4.0 × 10⁻⁷ m²  ·  ρ = 1.72 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m
HINT: L = RA / ρ
Answer: L ≈ 11.6 m
R3 — Find Resistivity
A wire 4 m long with cross-sectional area 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ m² has a measured resistance of 0.18 Ω. Identify the material by finding its resistivity.
L = 4 m  ·  A = 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ m²  ·  R = 0.18 Ω
HINT: ρ = RA/L — compare your answer to the table on Slide 3
Answer: ρ = 1.125 × 10⁻⁷ Ω·m (between Cu and Al)
R4 — Find Area
A nichrome heating element (ρ = 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m) must have R = 55 Ω and be 2.5 m long. What cross-sectional area is required?
R = 55 Ω  ·  L = 2.5 m  ·  ρ = 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m
HINT: A = ρL / R
Answer: A = 5.0 × 10⁻⁸ m²
★ R5 — CHALLENGE: Both Laws Together
A copper wire (ρ = 1.72 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m) is 10 m long with A = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ m². It is connected to a 6 V battery. (a) Find the resistance of the wire. (b) Find the current using Ohm's Law. (c) If the wire is replaced with one of double the length, what is the new current?
Use R = ρL/A first, then I = V/R
HINT: (a) R = (1.72 × 10⁻⁸ × 10) / (1.0 × 10⁻⁶)  ·  (b) I = 6/R  ·  (c) New R = 2 × old R
Answers: (a) R = 0.172 Ω  (b) I ≈ 34.9 A  (c) I ≈ 17.4 A
LESSON RECAP
V = IR
R = ρL/A
I = V/R
R = V/I
⚡
Resistance
Opposition to current flow. Symbol R, unit Ω. Depends on length, area, material, and temperature.
🔮
Resistivity ρ
Intrinsic material property (Ω·m). Low = good conductor (copper 1.72 × 10⁻⁸). High = insulator. Used in R = ρL/A.
📐
R = ρL/A
Resistance increases with length (R ∝ L) and decreases with cross-sectional area (R ∝ 1/A). Rearrange to find ρ, L, or A.

V–I Graph
Ohmic conductor: straight line through origin. Gradient = R. Non-ohmic (bulb, diode) = curved line.
